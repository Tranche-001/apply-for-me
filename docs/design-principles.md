# Design principles — apply-for-me

How we make UI decisions in this app. Three parts:

1. **Usability heuristics** — general rules of good interaction design (Nielsen).
2. **Product design principles** — values specific to *this* product, used to settle trade-offs.
3. **Decision log** — design decisions taken so far and the heuristic or principle behind each.

When justifying a design choice, cite the heuristic (e.g. `H6`) or principle (e.g. `P3`) instead of "because it looks better".

---

## 1. Ten usability heuristics

Source: Jakob Nielsen, *10 Usability Heuristics for User Interface Design*, Nielsen Norman Group, 1994 (last reviewed 2024-01-30). <https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/>

They are called heuristics because they are broad rules of thumb, not specific usability guidelines.

| # | Heuristic | What it means | In apply-for-me |
|---|-----------|---------------|-----------------|
| H1 | **Visibility of system status** | Keep users informed about what is going on, through appropriate feedback within a reasonable time. | Job flow shows its steps (Read → Compare → Ask → Draft → Save). Every AI action ends with a "What the AI did" report (new / reused / reordered / left out). Active filters show as chips plus "Showing X of Y blocks". |
| H2 | **Match between the system and the real world** | Speak the users' language; use familiar words and concepts, in a natural and logical order. | Use our own words consistently: *block*, *version*, *job*, *theme*. Behavioral questions stay in Portuguese, as they are asked in interviews. The CV preview looks like the real LaTeX output. |
| H3 | **User control and freedom** | Users act by mistake and need a clearly marked "emergency exit"; support undo and redo. | Every AI output is a draft you can edit, refine or discard. Cancelling a fresh duplicate removes the copy. Filter chips have ×. Style rules can be switched off. To build: undo for reorder and delete. |
| H4 | **Consistency and standards** | Users shouldn't wonder whether different words or actions mean the same thing; follow platform conventions. | Same card anatomy everywhere (title + three labeled bullets). Same PROJECT / STACK / IMPACT labels and colours on every screen. The same "Ask for changes" box wherever the AI writes. |
| H5 | **Error prevention** | Better than good error messages: remove error-prone conditions, or confirm before committing. | Theme input autocompletes, so you don't end up with both "Fintech" and "FinTech". The AI marks unknown facts with `[brackets]` instead of inventing them. Ask for confirmation before deleting a version that applications used. |
| H6 | **Recognition rather than recall** | Minimize memory load: make elements, actions and options visible. | Search by title, company or job. Version pills show their names. The theme picker lists every theme with counts. Each version shows "Used in …". |
| H7 | **Flexibility and efficiency of use** | Accelerators (hidden from novices) speed up experts; let users tailor frequent actions. | Drag *and* arrow buttons. Duplicate & edit. Reuse an existing version. Esc closes popovers; planned: `/` to focus search. |
| H8 | **Aesthetic and minimalist design** | No irrelevant or rarely needed information; every extra element competes with the relevant ones. | One "Themes" button instead of one pill per theme. Diff labels (TITLE CHANGED, CHANGED) only when something differs. The CV's "Show what changed" notes can be turned off. |
| H9 | **Help users recognize, diagnose and recover from errors** | Plain-language error messages, no codes; say precisely what went wrong and suggest a fix. | If a job URL can't be read (LinkedIn and others block automated reading), say so in plain words and offer "Paste the job text". If the AI call fails, keep the user's input and offer Retry. |
| H10 | **Help and documentation** | Best if no explanation is needed; otherwise help should be searchable, in context, task-focused and concrete. | Inline help where it's needed: "What interviewers look for", the Theory & references section, one-line hints under page titles. No separate manual. |

## 2. Product design principles

Source: Maria Rosala, *Crafting Product-Specific Design Principles to Support Better Decision Making*, Nielsen Norman Group, 2020-08-02. <https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-principles/>

**What they are:** value statements that describe the most important goals the product should deliver for its users, used to frame design decisions and keep them consistent. They are specific to one product, unlike heuristics (general), and they are not instructions for individual UI elements (those are patterns or standards).

**What makes a good one:**

- **Takes a stand** — names the value it favours, ideally over a value it often conflicts with.
- **Inspires empathy** — says why the value matters to users.
- **Concise** — short enough to reference and remember; not an essay.
- **Memorable** — few of them; 10 or more and most get forgotten.
- **Doesn't conflict with the others** — one value per principle; if two can clash, say which wins in which context.

**How to write them:** (1) identify the core values, (2) ask how each value affects users, (3) settle the common trade-offs explicitly, (4) write several versions, compare, iterate. Then keep them visible and *use them to justify decisions* ("P3 tips the balance"), not authority or taste.

### Our principles (draft, to be reviewed and rewritten by Guilherme)

- **P1 — Truth over polish.** The AI never invents a fact; anything it can't confirm becomes a `[bracket]` or a question to you. *Why:* everything on the CV can be asked about in an interview.
- **P2 — You decide, the AI drafts.** Every AI output is an editable draft, and every AI action says what it changed. Control wins over automation. *Why:* it's your name on the CV, and the learning loop depends on your edits.
- **P3 — Reuse over regenerate.** Prefer an existing version; write a new one only when none fits. *Why:* across 300+ applications you need a library you can find things in and trust.
- **P4 — Built for the 300th application.** When speed for a frequent task conflicts with first-time clarity, favour the frequent task (search, duplicate, keyboard), but never hide what a control does. *Why:* applying is repetitive work; seconds saved per application add up.
